## Design rules (one look for the whole deck)
- 16:9. One idea per slide. Font: DM Sans or any clean sans (Calibri/Arial is fine). Sizes: title 40 pt, body 24-28 pt, never below 18 pt.
- Colours: dark teal `#0F2A33` (title and closing slides), light `#F4F7F6` (content), teal `#17687A` for RL / accents, **orange `#B85A0B` always means "the tuned fixed schedule / the baseline to beat"**. Blue vs orange stays readable for colour-blind viewers.

## Slide 0 - Title
**On the slide:**
- Title: *Can reinforcement learning share scarce water fairly?*
- Subtitle: Equity-aware zone scheduling for intermittent water supply, tested in simulation against a tuned fixed schedule
- Footer: [Team names] · [Course] · 2 November 2026
**Say (20 s):** "Many cities supply water only a few hours a day. We asked whether a learned policy can decide which areas get water, so that more water is served and the worst-off area is protected. Short answer at the end: sometimes, not always, and we can say exactly when."

## Slide 1 - The problem
**Title:** Water arrives for a few hours, so who gets it first matters
**On the slide (three cards):**
1. *Supply is rationed* - zones receive water only some hours per day.
2. *Households store water* - roof tanks fill when water arrives; the utility cannot see their levels.
3. *Tanks can widen inequity* - households with better hydraulic access fill first (Hendrickson & Sela, 2024).
**Bottom line (large):** Can a learned policy serve more water and protect the worst-off zone?
**Visual:** none needed; optional simple icon row. Footer: Hendrickson & Sela (2024).
**Say (60 s):** explain intermittent supply; give the decision (each hour, which zones are open); stress the **hidden household tanks**: the utility sees pressure and flow but not what is in the tanks. *Do not invent a city example; if you want one, cite a source you have actually read.*

## Slide 2 - What already exists
**Title:** Optimised schedules exist; we did not find RL for equity under intermittent supply
**On the slide (two columns):**
- *Closest prior work - optimisation:* Hendrickson & Sela (2024): Bayesian-optimised valve schedules, household tanks, equity metric. Kurian et al. (2023): mixed-integer schedule for rural networks. [A 2026 npj Clean Water paper on equitable intermittent supply is reported to exist; **read it before presenting**.]
- *RL for water networks:* pump scheduling (Pei 2025; Hajgato 2020), pressure control (Belfadil 2024), valve scheduling for contamination (Hu 2020), pump+valve cost (Hu 2023). Objectives are cost or pressure, not equity.
- *Fair RL theory:* Siddique et al. (2020), generalized Gini welfare.
**Bottom note:** "In our search we found no RL paper combining intermittent supply, zone scheduling, equity and household storage. Search was not exhaustive."
**Say (60 s):** position the work. Be explicit that the closest work is *optimisation*, so our fair baseline is an optimised fixed schedule. Say "in our search", never "first". **Never** claim novelty over the 2026 npj paper until someone has read it.
**Source:** `docs/related_work.md` section 7.

## Slide 3 - The environment
**Title:** A 4-zone network with hourly open/close decisions
**On the slide (six small cards):**
- *Network:* EPANET Net3, 59 demand nodes, 4 zones.
- *Decision:* each hour choose which zones are open (16 combinations).
- *Hidden state:* household roof tanks (0.3-0.8 days of use), not observed.
- *Budget:* daily source volume 50-90% of demand, known one day ahead.
- *Reward:* minus unmet demand each hour, plus an end-of-day bonus for the worst zone (weight λ).
- *Episode:* 3 days = 72 decisions; hydraulics by EPANET pressure-dependent demand.
**Say (75 s):** walk through one hour: pick zones → households request water to refill tanks (up to alpha times normal demand) → EPANET computes what actually arrives given pressure → tanks fill, households draw → reward. Mention **partial observability** (hidden tanks). State that tank sizes and surge factor are assumptions.
**Footer:** Simulation only; assumptions in `docs/assumptions.md`.

## Slide 4 - Why v2
**Title:** The first version was too easy, so we made the hydraulics bind
**On the slide (two columns):**
- *v1:* mean supply capped near 0.773 by the water budget; PPO reached 0.767, 99% of the ceiling; pressure thresholds 10/20/30 m gave identical results.
- *v2 (demand ×8):* opening every zone at once now starves the network; pressure thresholds matter; methods spread from 0.56 to 0.72 mean supply.
**Say (50 s):** this is a methodological point worth making: we diagnosed that the benchmark was saturated (a result near the budget ceiling cannot show a method is good) and changed it before drawing conclusions. All results that follow are v2.

## Slide 5 - Methods compared
**Title:** One test set, many methods
**On the slide (table):**
| Group | Methods | Runs |
|---|---|---|
| Simple rules | random, all-open, rota, greedy k1/k2 | 1 |
| Privileged rule | greedy using hidden tank levels (not deployable) | 1 |
| **Tuned fixed daily schedule** | cross-entropy search, 40 iterations, population 60 | 3 |
| Reinforcement learning | PPO, PPO + domain randomisation, DQN, PPO without equity term | 5 seeds each |
| Recurrent RL | LSTM PPO, LSTM PPO + randomisation | 3 seeds each |
| Equity-weight sweep | PPO with λ = 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4 | 2-5 seeds |
| Oracle | hindsight upper bound, 30 scenarios | - |
**Note line:** Test scenarios use seeds 10000+, never used in training. 100 scenarios (default), 50 per other condition.
**Say (60 s):** emphasise the **tuned fixed schedule** is the strong baseline an expert would build (a 24-hour timetable repeated daily, optimised on training scenarios). The oracle is only a reference: it sees the future.

## Slide 6 - Main result
**Title:** On matching conditions, the tuned schedule is hard to beat
**On the slide:** table (default condition, 100 scenarios; mean supply and worst zone with 95% CI)
| Method | mean supply | worst zone |
|---|---|---|
| **Tuned fixed schedule (3)** | 0.716 [0.706, 0.725] | **0.630** [0.623, 0.638] |
| PPO (5) | 0.716 [0.707, 0.725] | 0.601 [0.592, 0.609] |
| PPO + domain randomisation (5) | 0.711 [0.703, 0.720] | 0.612 [0.604, 0.621] |
| DQN (5) | 0.714 [0.705, 0.723] | 0.590 [0.582, 0.597] |
| PPO, no equity term (5) | 0.718 [0.709, 0.727] | 0.564 [0.553, 0.574] |
| Random | 0.672 [0.664, 0.680] | 0.527 [0.518, 0.537] |
**Callout (paired vs tuned schedule, M1):** mean supply: tie · worst zone: PPO 0.030 lower (CI 0.028-0.031) · return: PPO 0.52 lower (CI 0.46-0.59).
**Optional figure:** `plots/out/bars_ci.png` (all 18 methods; busy: use as backup).
**Say (90 s):** read the table in two sentences: "RL matches the tuned timetable on how much water is delivered, but not on protecting the worst zone." Point out that removing the equity term makes the worst zone clearly worse (0.564), so the equity term does something. Mention the checks: every PPO run is below every tuned run on worst zone.
**Source footer:** results_v2/summary.csv, paired_vs_ce_tuned.csv.

## Slide 7 - How much headroom is left?
**Title:** Little headroom is left above the tuned schedule
**On the slide (three big numbers):** Oracle return **-8.49** · Tuned schedule **-9.15** · PPO **-9.67**
**Below:** mean supply: oracle 0.725 vs tuned 0.716; worst zone: oracle 0.631 vs tuned 0.630.
**Caveat line (must be on the slide):** Oracle uses 30 scenarios and sees the future; the other methods use 100 scenarios.
**Say (50 s):** the oracle is an upper bound that knows future budgets and hidden tanks. The tuned schedule is already close to it on supply; some gap remains on the training objective (return). Do **not** state a "% of gap closed"; the samples differ.

## Slide 8 - The equity weight
**Title:** A larger equity weight lifts the worst zone at a small cost
**Figure (left):** `plots/out/pareto_lambda.png` (PPO λ = 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, with the tuned schedule, DQN, PPO+DR).
**Text (right):**
- Worst zone: 0.573 → 0.635 as λ goes 0 → 4.
- Mean supply: 0.718 → 0.708.
- λ = 4 reaches the tuned schedule's worst zone (0.635 vs 0.630) for about 0.008 less mean supply.
**Honesty line:** 2-5 seeds per point, not monotone (λ = 2 below λ = 1), intervals overlap. λ is a **reward penalty, not a constraint**.
**Say (60 s):** λ is the knob that trades efficiency for fairness. Do not call it "Lagrangian" or "constrained RL". Our bonus is a max-min-style term and is **not** the generalized-Gini optimisation of Siddique et al.; say that if asked.

## Slide 9 - Robustness under shift
**Title:** RL helps only when conditions move away from the tuning
**On the slide (table; return vs the tuned schedule, M1's paired CI):**
| Condition | PPO | PPO + DR | DQN |
|---|---|---|---|
| default | worse (-0.52) | worse (-0.77) | worse (-0.86) |
| alpha = 2 | **better (+1.14)** | **better (+1.66)** | **better (+2.01)** |
| alpha = 8 | worse (-0.20) | tie (+0.08) | worse (-0.81) |
| small tanks | tie (-0.15) | tie (+0.05) | **better (+1.14)** |
| other 6 conditions* | worse | worse | worse |
*abundant, five days, large tanks, noisy demand, 10 m pressure, 30 m pressure.
**Side notes:** alpha = 2 lies outside the randomisation range (3-6). The schedule was tuned on the default condition only. LSTM PPO is below PPO; LSTM + randomisation is much worse on the worst zone (0.469): a negative result.
**Say (75 s):** a fixed timetable cannot adapt when the surge cap changes; a reactive policy can. That is the one place RL wins clearly. Overall PPO is worse in 8 of 10 non-scarce conditions, better in 1, tied in 1. Scarce supply is excluded because every method scores about the same there.

## Slide 12 - Limits
**Title:** What this study cannot claim (six cards)
1. One simulated network, no field data.
2. Tank sizes, surge factor and budgets are assumed (0.3-0.8 days is probably below real storage).
3. 2-5 training seeds per method; oracle on 30 scenarios.
4. The fixed schedule was tuned on one condition.
5. The equity weight is a reward penalty, not a constraint.
6. Literature checked mostly at abstract level; forward-citation search not done.
**Say (50 s):** examiners respect this slide. Say what each limit would change.

## Slide 13 - Conclusion
**Title:** What we can say
- A tuned fixed daily schedule matches PPO on mean supply and beats it on worst-zone supply under matching conditions.
- RL wins under a changed surge cap (alpha = 2) and, for DQN, with small tanks.
- A larger equity weight raises worst-zone supply for a small loss in mean supply.
**Not claimed:** "first", "RL beats optimised scheduling" without "under the shifts we tested", "constrained RL".
**Next:** second network · measured tank sizes · fairness as a constraint · preference-conditioned policy.
**Say (45 s):** repeat the three bullets, then the one-line takeaway: "Use a good timetable by default; use RL where conditions change."

## Questions you may get (short answers)
1. **Why not use the oracle?** It needs the future budget and hidden tank levels; it is a bound.
2. **Why is the tuned schedule so good?** Demand and supply repeat daily, a daily timetable captures that, and hidden tank state is the only thing RL could exploit but cannot observe.
3. **Did you tune the RL?** Not systematically (one PPO setting, one DQN setting). A tuned RL baseline could change the picture; say so.
4. **Is 5 seeds enough?** It is thin (literature suggests more). We report interval estimates over scenarios and checked that every PPO run is below every schedule run on the default condition; seed variance is not in the CIs.
5. **What does 0.03 on the worst zone mean?** Three percentage points of one zone's supply; statistically clear on our scenarios, practically modest.
6. **Why the worst zone as the equity measure?** Max-min fairness over zones. Gini and Jain indices are in the data and could rank methods differently.
7. **Is your equity term the same as Siddique et al.?** No; theirs is generalized-Gini welfare over per-user returns. Ours is a daily bonus on the worst-zone ratio.
8. **Why does the privileged greedy do badly?** We measured it (0.639 mean supply, 0.494 worst zone) but did not investigate why; one untested guess is that always serving the single emptiest zone causes large refill surges that waste budget and pressure.
9. **Is your simulator realistic?** It is a stylised model on a benchmark network; hydraulics are real EPANET, household behaviour and tank sizes are assumed.
10. **Is this new?** "In our search we did not find RL for equity under intermittent supply." We have not read the 2026 npj paper.
11. **Why v2?** v1 was saturated (PPO at 99% of the ceiling); v2 makes hydraulics bind.
12. **Why did the LSTM not help?** It is a negative result: more parameters, same data; with randomisation it also hurt the worst zone. We did not investigate the cause.
13. **What would you do next?** Second network, measured tank sizes, a constrained or preference-conditioned formulation, more seeds.
14. **Any bugs?** The oracle's first version scored below PPO, which exposed a weak search; fixed with warm starts. Several training runs crashed on Windows (EPANET temp files); no results were affected.
